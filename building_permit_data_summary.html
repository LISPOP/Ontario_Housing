Available Building Permit Data

Introduction

The general idea was to see if we can use published building permits to identify local projects of densification to see if respondents in our Ontario 2022 survey who have experienced densification in their neighbourhood differ in their attitudes to housing policy than those who have not.

The starting point is our own survey, which has respondents across certain municipalities in Ontario. This is the count of respondents that we have by municipality.

However, it’s more complicated. There are four major decisions to make. Lurking behind all of these are sample size questions, because we only have so many building permits from so many municipalities and we only have so many respondents and they have to match up.

Which municipalities to we use?

The municipalities do not always collect the same data. For example, some municipalities track the number of units a development adds, others do not. We have to map similar variables to similar variables. This is not a super challenge, the questions below are more pressing and complicated.

Variables Available by Municipality

One of the problems is that each file collects somewhat different information and stores them with differet variable names. Table 1 summarizes the variables that are available in these building-permit data.

Decision 1. Do we use building permit issue date or building permit data?

Some municipalities track the former, fewer track both. Advantages of using building issue date are that we have more municipalities and it might meaningfully spur NIMBY-ism. Disadvantages include that not all building permits are ever completed, and they may not be noticed in the neighbourhood and thus may not spur any kind of attitude change.

Decision 2. How do we measure densification?

Some city variables have a variable that measures units created, but not all (e.g. Hamilton does not). PLus, municipalities have other variables that differently capture what the building permit is meant to do. How do we combine these to come up with some kind of measure of projects that can be called densification.

Decision 3 Do we define densification in a relative way, relative to the repsondent’s dissemination area?

Related to questio two, we have at our hand the density of each respondent’s built environment (e.g. percent of the respondent’s dissemination area that is buildings larger than five stories). So, some of our respondents live in single-family home DAs, some live in areas that are almost all towers. Do we care about public opinion about a tower dweller living in a neighbourhood when another tower goes up? Do we care about a person living in a moderately dense neighbourhood feels about densification when a tower goes up? My thought was that it might make sense to take the clean case of a respondent who lives in a zero-tower neighbourhood and then has a moderate densification project (1 to 6 units). Then, by definition, you have respondents who are facing more densification than previously. Plus, this is clearly a public policy concern as that is where densification has to happen the most and where it meets the most objections. It basically sets the tower question aside, but this does come at the cost of sample size.

The building permit data includes information on everything from decks, to small conversions adding suites to large towers.

Decision 4 Which time frame do we use?

If we are interested in the causal effect of some kind of densification project in a respondent’s neighbourhood, how closely does the project have to be prior to the survey to have some kind of effect? Six months? 1 year? The longer the time frame, the more building permits we will have, but might the causal effect of a building permit be lost, the longer we extend the time frame?

Summary of trade-offs

This section summarizes what the data say about each of the four questions. The numbers come from the analysis in the sections below. They are typed in rather than calculated here, so they need updating if the data or the flow chart change. Unless stated otherwise, permits are those issued or completed between January 2021 and April 2022, and the survey was in the field from May 18 to 27, 2022. A “zero-tower” respondent lives in a DA where no dwellings are in buildings of five or more storeys.

1. Issue date or completed date

  • Completed date: four cities (Toronto, Mississauga, Hamilton and Kitchener), with 684 respondents, 293 of them zero-tower.
  • Issue date: six cities have permits issued in the window, with 844 respondents, 397 of them zero-tower. Nearly all of the gain is Ottawa (133 respondents, 84 zero-tower). St. Catharines adds 27 (20 zero-tower), but it records neither units created nor structure type. Burlington adds nothing either way, because its file starts in June 2022, after the survey.
  • Not every issued permit is finished. Of the permits issued in the window, 91% in Mississauga and 82% in Kitchener had been completed by the time the files were downloaded. In Hamilton, whose completion dates only run to January 2024, the figure is 66%. Toronto’s rate is unknown because its file only includes permits that have been closed.
  • “Completed” does not always mean built. In Toronto, the completed date is the date the permit was closed, and that includes cancelled permits. Of the 2,567 Toronto permits creating at least one unit that closed in the window, 407 (16%) were cancelled. The flow chart does not currently drop them.
  • The two dates pick up different stages of a project. For permits adding 1 to 6 units that were completed in the window, the median time from issue to completion was about 8 months in Mississauga (254 days), 21 months in Toronto (635 days, closed permits only) and 23 months in Kitchener (693 days). Most were issued before 2021: 1,694 of 2,108 in Toronto, 443 of 761 in Mississauga and 1,317 of 1,517 in Kitchener. So a project completed shortly before the survey has usually been under construction, and visible to neighbours, for a year or more. A project issued shortly before the survey was mostly still under construction, or not yet started, when respondents took the survey.

The trade-off: the issue date adds Ottawa, about 160 respondents in total, and captures the moment neighbours may first react. The completed date marks a project that has actually changed the neighbourhood, but in Toronto it needs cancelled permits removed, and it limits us to four cities.

2. Measuring densification

  • Units created are recorded in Toronto, Ottawa, Mississauga and Kitchener (and Burlington, which has no permits in the window). Hamilton and St. Catharines do not record them, so their projects can only be classified from the permit category and the work description.
  • Densification projects found by the flow chart (addresses, completed in the window): Toronto 933, Mississauga 545, Kitchener 387 and Hamilton 250.
  • Each city reaches “densification” through a different variable. In Mississauga, 532 of the 545 projects come from the permit type (a unit added to an existing building). In Toronto and Kitchener, most come from the structure type (a multi-unit or added-suite building). Hamilton’s projects are almost all conversions (236 of 250), and because Hamilton has no unit counts, the 1-to-6-unit limit cannot be applied there. “Convert Multiple to Multiple” may include large projects.
  • Many projects are unclassified. New construction with no demolition at the same address is marked “Unclear”. These may be new subdivisions on vacant land rather than infill: Kitchener has 1,033 such addresses (more than its 387 densification projects), Hamilton 756, Toronto 261 and Mississauga 124. How they are treated changes the Kitchener and Hamilton counts a lot.
  • Projects with more than 6 units are rare: 69 in Toronto, 28 in Kitchener and 11 in Mississauga.
  • The flow chart has only been applied to completed permits. Using the issue date would mean extending it to Ottawa (which has unit counts) and St. Catharines (which would rely on its permit categories).

The trade-off: a rule based on units created is consistent and easy to defend, but it drops Hamilton and St. Catharines. A rule based on permit and structure categories covers every city, but each city needs its own mapping, so the measure means something slightly different from city to city.

3. Densification and neighbourhood density

  • Restricting to zero-tower respondents keeps 420 of 871 (48%) in the seven cities, and 293 of 684 (43%) in the four completed-date cities. Another 51 respondents have no tower-share value for their DA.
  • The restriction falls hardest on Toronto: 172 of 481 Toronto respondents (36%) are zero-tower, compared with 60% to 89% in the other cities. After the restriction, Toronto drops from 55% of the sample to 41%, even though most densification projects are in Toronto.
  • The exact cut-off barely matters. Relaxing “zero” to “less than 10%” adds only 24 respondents (444 instead of 420), because respondents mostly live either in DAs with no towers or in DAs that are mostly towers.
  • Still unknown: how many zero-tower respondents have a 1-to-6-unit project in their neighbourhood. That depends on linking permit locations to respondents’ DAs, which has not been done yet. It is the number that sets the size of the “treated” group. Hamilton’s permit file has no locations, and Toronto permits have to be matched to address points.

The trade-off: the zero-tower restriction gives a clean case (every nearby project is new density for these respondents) that matches where densification meets the most opposition. It costs about half the sample and most of Toronto, and it means we can say nothing about how residents of denser neighbourhoods react to new projects.

4. Time frame

Densification projects (addresses) by how long before the survey their last permit was completed:

  • The count grows roughly in proportion to the window: six months gives half as many projects as twelve.
  • The window interacts with the choice of date. Because a typical project takes 8 to 23 months from issue to completion, a “6 months before the survey, completed date” project was usually under construction for a year or more before that. A short completion window does not mean a short exposure.
  • Sixteen months is the longest window the current code loads. The Toronto and Hamilton files go back to 2017, so longer windows are possible for the completed-date cities. Only Ottawa’s 2021 and 2022 files are loaded (the 2020 file is commented out), so a longer issue-date window would need that file.

The trade-off: a longer window gives more projects and so more treated respondents. But it also includes projects whose effect may have faded, and respondents who moved in after a project was built and so never experienced it as a change.

Building Permits Issued

The analysis so far uses the date a permit was completed, which limits us to the four cities that record one. All seven cities record the date a permit was issued, so Table 4 counts the permits each city issued over the same window (January 2021 to April 2022), alongside the permits completed in that window where a completed date exists.

There are several caveats to counting permits by issue date:

  • Burlington: the file only begins in June 2022, so it contributes no permits issued in this window.
  • St. Catharines and Hamilton: neither records the number of dwelling units created, so their issued permits can only be classified using the permit category and the free-text work description.
  • Toronto: the file contains cleared permits, meaning permits that have since been closed. Permits issued in this window that are still open, or were never finished, are missing, so Toronto’s issued count is an undercount.
  • Ottawa: a permit covering several addresses (e.g., a block of townhouses) appears once per address, with the permit’s total units repeated on each row, so the table counts each permit and its units once. Large Ottawa apartment projects are also often split into a foundation-only permit and a later permit for the rest of the building, so some units may be counted on more than one permit.
  • Issued is not built: some issued permits are later cancelled or never completed, so counts based on issue date overstate the work that actually happened on the ground.

Structure Type Explained

The BLG TYPE variable in the City of Ottawa’s building permit data identifies the type of structure each permit applies to. It covers residential buildings (single-detached houses, semi-detached houses, duplexes, coach houses, rowhouses, stacked rowhouses and apartment buildings), non-residential buildings (retail, office, commercial, industrial, institutional, mixed-use and farm buildings) and a few other categories, including accessory structures such as detached garages and sheds, and demolitions.

The STRUCTURE_TYPE variable in the City of Toronto’s cleared building permit data identifies the type of building a permit applies to. Toronto’s variable is highly detailed, with 187 distinct categories. They range from residential forms (single-family dwellings that are detached, semi-detached or townhouses; two-unit and three-plus-unit houses; laneway and rear-yard suites; stacked townhouses; multiple-unit buildings; and apartment buildings) to a long list of specific non-residential uses such as offices, retail stores, restaurants, schools, hospitals, warehouses and transit stations.

The BLDG_TYPE variable in the City of Mississauga’s building permit data identifies the type of building each permit applies to. It uses a short, standardized list of 27 categories, covering residential buildings (detached and semi-detached dwellings, row dwellings, condominium and street townhouses, duplexes through sixplexes, and apartment buildings of more than six units), commercial and industrial buildings (split into single-user and multi-user retail, office and industrial buildings), and public and institutional buildings such as schools, churches and government facilities.

The PERMITCLAS (permit class) variable in the City of Hamilton’s building and demolition permit data identifies the type of building or structure each permit applies to. It is a detailed classification with 139 categories. Residential types are defined mostly by the number of households (single-family, two-family and three-family dwellings, semi-detached dwellings, townhouses, multiple dwellings and apartments), alongside a long list of specific non-residential uses such as stores, offices, restaurants, warehouses, schools and hospitals.

The Sub Work Type variable in the City of Kitchener’s building permit data describes the specific type of building or structure each permit covers. It has 154 categories. Unlike a pure building-type field, it mixes types of dwelling (single detached, semi-detached, duplex, rowhouse and apartment, often split into condo and non-condo versions) with the specific item being built or installed, such as decks, swimming pools, sheds, garages, signs and plumbing systems.

The City of St. Catharines’ building permit data do not include a variable that identifies the type of building. The closest equivalent is FOLDERDESC, which records the broad category of each permit. Its 14 categories largely follow the Ontario Building Code’s major occupancy groups (residential, assembly, mercantile, business and personal services, industrial and institutional), plus permit types such as plumbing, signs, secondary buildings and temporary structures.

The SUBDESC variable in the City of Burlington’s building permit data identifies the type of building or structure each permit applies to. It has 64 categories, covering residential forms (single detached and semi-detached dwellings, row and stacked townhouses, fourplexes, apartments and accessory dwelling units), commercial, industrial and institutional buildings, and accessory structures such as decks, garages and tents.

Conclusion and concerns

Mostly this works quite well. However, the Hamilton data might be concerning because it does not actually count the number of units created. The category of Convert multiple to multiple might actually include large-scale projects.
